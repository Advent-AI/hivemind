# Resolvable questions and evaluation rubrics: forecasting-community question standards and 2024–2026 benchmarks that grade AI analyst output

Scope of this note: (a) the written rules forecasting platforms and prediction markets use to decide whether a question is worth asking and can be resolved; (b) how 2025–2026 prediction markets phrase company-fundamental contracts; (c) every 2024–2026 benchmark found that grades LLM-written theses, equity research or financial research reports (unit judged, rubric dimensions, who labels, agreement numbers, judge failure modes); (d) how human gold is built for subjective analytical tasks. Researched 2026-10-26. Quotes marked "verbatim" were returned as quoted strings from the page; text marked "paraphrase" is the fetch tool's summary of the page and should be re-checked before being quoted in a design document. Metaculus pages were read through a reader proxy because metaculus.com returns 403 to direct fetches.

## Key question 1. What makes a question "resolvable" and "decision-relevant" in these standards? Quote the tests.

### Takeaway
Every platform converges on the same test: a question is resolvable when a named, pre-committed source and a written procedure decide YES/NO/N/A on an explicit date with no discretion left to the resolver, and edge cases and fallbacks are written in advance. "Decision-relevant" is handled separately (Good Judgment's "rigor–relevance trade-off"; FutureSearch's "consequential"/"decision-relevance" desiderata) and is the harder half: no platform has a mechanical test for it, and Good Judgment's answer is to recover relevance by clustering several rigorous questions rather than loosening any one question.

### Cited Findings

**Metaculus (question-writing guide; read via proxy; verbatim quotes)**
- "The resolution criteria should leave little room for discretion in deciding the resolution." — [Metaculus question-writing guide](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- "Try to limit the scope for ex-post quarrels about what really happened, and who was right." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- "Define your terms. Questions of the sort 'will X occur?' often hinge on how X is defined." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- "Try to specify precisely and in detail which steps should or shouldn't be followed when resolving the question." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- "Use authoritative sources, when possible. Good options are numerical data regularly published by a reliable publicly available source." Authors must ensure "that the sources will be available at the time of resolution, or otherwise you might want to specify alternative sources of information." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- "Consider and account for edge-cases. Try to imagine scenarios for which the resolution conditions fail to cleanly apply." / "Consider fall-back criteria. When you have a resolution that should be easy to check assuming all goes well, try to handle also the case where all doesn't go well." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- "The date or time period the question is asking about must always be explicitly mentioned in the text." / "Delegate the boring stuff to the fine-print." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- Rejection tests (verbatim): questions are rejected if they "would require more than approximately 15 minutes of Admin effort to resolve"; "Questions which are not nearly ready to publish and which do not follow the guidelines on this page will be rejected." — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing/)
- From the search snippet of the same page (paraphrase): questions should have at least 2 paragraphs or ~100 words of context unless self-explanatory or high-value and urgent; all dates must be absolute, not relative ("in this century", "after the opening of this question"). — [Metaculus](https://www.metaculus.com/question-writing)
- Metaculus FAQ, resolution outcomes (paraphrase with embedded verbatim fragments): **Ambiguous** applies when "reality is not clear" (conflicting or unavailable reporting); **Annulled** applies when "reality is clear but the question is not". Three annulment causes: *underspecified* questions (the question "did not clearly specify how" the outcome would be determined); *subverted assumptions* (an unstated assumption proved false, e.g. an organization expected to publish data never did, or a methodology changed); *imbalanced outcomes* (a binary question with no clear mechanism for both Yes and No, so only one outcome or annulment is plausible). Platform appropriateness requires questions to be "unambiguously resolvable" with "tangible, objective facts about the world which are well-defined and not a matter of opinion." — [Metaculus FAQ](https://www.metaculus.com/help/faq/)

**Manifold Markets (creator guide; verbatim quotes)**
- Strong questions are "Falsifiable: there should be a clear way to know YES vs NO when the time comes." / "Specific: 'Will an AI model score over 90% on benchmark X by date Y?' beats 'Will AI get smarter this year?'" / "Time-boxed: a deadline avoids markets that drift forever." — [Manifold creator guide](https://manifold.markets/community-guidelines/creator-guide)
- "Spell out what counts as YES, what counts as NO, and what counts as N/A — before traders bet." / "Name the source you'll use (e.g. 'official results from organization X')." / "Cover the edge cases you can think of — event cancelled, source unavailable, ambiguous outcome." — [Manifold](https://manifold.markets/community-guidelines/creator-guide)
- "Avoid the word 'by' with dates — it's ambiguous. Use 'before [date]' if you mean before that day begins, or 'before the end of [date]' if you mean by 11:59pm." — [Manifold](https://manifold.markets/community-guidelines/creator-guide)
- Manifold's own community notes that "there is currently an optional field for context or details, and there is no direct incentive for the creator to provide any criteria for resolution" and that "Most Manifold markets are probably too imprecise and ambiguous"; also that "the more one tries to nail down resolution criteria, the more likely there is a mismatch between the top-line summary and the detailed decision tree" (paraphrase from search snippets of a Manifold market discussing mandatory criteria). — [Manifold discussion market](https://manifold.markets/Primer/resolution-criteria-mandatory-for-y-RAI5qtUp0g)

**Good Judgment (question clusters page; verbatim)**
- "But in checking all the boxes to craft a rigorous question, narrow topics may lose their relevance to decision-makers trying to determine the prospects for their business down the line." — [Good Judgment, Question Clustering](https://goodjudgment.com/question_clusters/)

**FutureSearch (Bosse, Mühlbacher, Wildman, Phillips, Schwarz), "Automating Forecasting Question Generation and Resolution for AI Evaluation", arXiv 2601.22444, January 2026 (paraphrase of the fetched HTML; the quoted fragments were returned as quotes)**
- Five desiderata for a forecasting question: (1) "Unambiguously resolvable" — "clear, objective resolution criteria that can be verified"; (2) accurately resolved; (3) difficult — performance should improve with intelligence and effort; (4) consequential — exercises generalizable forecasting capabilities; (5) diverse. Appendix A.2 adds non-extreme base rates, decision-relevance, appropriate difficulty distribution, independent outcomes. — [arXiv 2601.22444](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22444)
- Four verifier agents gate each generated question: a quality verifier (non-trivial forecasting task; must rate "great"), an ambiguity verifier (must rate "great"), a resolvability verifier (autonomous AI resolution possible; must rate "very certainly yes"), a forecast verifier (produces a probability to flag triviality). 2,500 seeds → 1,793 questions passed all verifiers → 1,499 after embedding/DBSCAN deduplication. — [arXiv 2601.22444](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22444)
- Human check of question quality: on 149 sampled questions, human expert ratings were 75.2% "accept", 16.8% "soft reject", 8.1% "hard reject". Resolution accuracy: an expert forecaster resolved a random 100; the automated resolver made 4 errors (2 wrong binaries, 2 missed annulments), estimated 4.9% error rate; human resolution took "an average of 13 minutes per question". Estimated annulment rate "approximately 3.9% (95% CI: [1.1%, 8.4%])" vs "Metaculus' historical rate of approximately 8%". The abstract claims "verifiable, unambiguous questions approximately 96% of the time, exceeding the rate of Metaculus." — [arXiv 2601.22444 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22444) and [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22444)
- Note: the search result labelled this an FRI paper; the paper's authors are at FutureSearch, not the Forecasting Research Institute. — [arXiv 2601.22444](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22444)

**ForecastBench (Karger et al., Forecasting Research Institute; arXiv 2409.19839, Sept 2024; ICLR 2025) — question inclusion rules (paraphrase with verbatim fragments)**
- Market questions are taken only where there is active trading: "We choose those with high levels of active human trading on these platforms (liquidity) as these questions tend to be of higher quality than those with low levels of human trading." — [ForecastBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19839)
- Dataset questions (ACLED, DBnomics, FRED, Wikipedia, Yahoo! Finance) are templated comparisons at fixed horizons, e.g. "What is the probability that the daily average temperature at the French weather station at Abbeville will be higher on resolution_date than on forecast_due_date?", with horizons n∈{7,30,90,180,365,1095,1825,3650} days. Nightly updates drop "invalid, low-quality, and resolved questions". Question bank 6,435; each round 1,000 questions = 500 standard + 500 "combination questions". — [ForecastBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19839)

**Community critique of ambiguity (older: LessWrong, 2022; paraphrase with verbatim fragments)**
- Core claim: when criteria are unclear traders predict "the psychology of whoever evaluates the market" rather than the outcome. Rejected examples: a Russia chemical-weapons market with "no fine print as to what happens if e.g. Ukraine alleges Russian use of chemical weapons, Russia denies it, the UN says 'this is a serious accusation and we are forming an investigative committee'"; a Belarus extradition market with "No mention of what counts as 'en masse'. 100 people? 1,000? 10,000?" and undefined "reputable Russian or Belorussian sources". Proposed rules: "If you are outsourcing market evaluation to some external source, make it clear which one"; "If part of market evaluation needs to be based in your personal judgment, at least make it clear that this is the case, and make it clear under which circumstances you will resolve the market which way." — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/DpDnXHcPejd9tn8R5/ambiguity-in-prediction-market-resolution-is-harmful)

### Inferences
- The platform tests reduce to five checkable fields a gold question can carry: (1) metric named exactly as the source reports it ("only the specified metric… alternate versions that differ in definition or scope will not be considered"); (2) a named primary source and a named fallback; (3) an absolute date, phrased "before [date]" or "before the end of [date]", never "by"; (4) a written rule for each of Yes/No/N-A including no-report, range, and revision cases; (5) a resolution-effort cap (Metaculus: ~15 minutes). A question missing any field is a "soft reject" in FutureSearch's terms.
- Prediction markets phrase company fundamentals almost exclusively as *threshold ladders on a single reported line item for a single fiscal period* (data-center revenue > $50B / $65B in Q1 FY27), *rank/state on a date* (largest company by market cap on June 30), or *speech acts* (word mentioned on the analyst call). None found phrase a thesis-level question (e.g. "will hyperscaler capex growth decelerate"). This is exactly the "rigorous but narrow" end of Good Judgment's trade-off; the market's job is settlement, not relevance.
- Metaculus's three annulment causes (underspecified, subverted assumption, imbalanced outcomes) are a ready-made negative rubric for grading an extracted question: each is a defect class that can be counted on a gold set.

### Gaps
- Kalshi's exact rule text for EPS/revenue "beat" markets (how consensus is sourced, which filing is authoritative, revision handling) was not captured: kalshi.com returned HTTP 429/403 on every fetch and no mirror carried the rule text. Only the mention-market rules were recovered.
- Good Judgment Inc's internal question-writing guideline document (beyond the public question-clusters page) was not found; Good Judgment Open's help pages were not reachable.
- The Polymarket rule text was read from a third-party mirror, not polymarket.com; wording should be verified on the live market before being quoted as Polymarket's.

## Key question 2. How do they avoid questions that are trivially fine (one line item) or hopelessly coarse ("will AI win")? Any explicit guidance on decomposing a big question into a cluster of dated sub-questions?

### Takeaway
The explicit decomposition method is Good Judgment/Tetlock's "question clusters" (also "Bayesian question clusters"): keep each sub-question rigorous and dated, and make the *set* carry relevance by covering the strategic question "from multiple perspectives". Triviality is filtered by a forecast-based check (FutureSearch's forecast verifier, "non-extreme base rates"), by liquidity (ForecastBench), and by requiring that resolution depend on the thing that matters rather than a side condition (LessWrong's "trivial pathways" and "failed parameter tuning").

### Cited Findings
- Good Judgment defines clusters as "sets of questions about discrete events that, in combination, shed light on a broader topic", which "examine the strategic question from multiple perspectives—political, economic, financial, security, and informational." (verbatim) — [Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com/question_clusters/)
- Good Judgment's Taiwan cluster (six questions) includes (verbatim): "Will Taiwan accuse the People's Republic of China (PRC) of flying a military aircraft over the territory of the main island of Taiwan without permission before 31 December 2021?" and "Before 1 January 2022, will US legislation explicitly authorizing the president to use the armed forces to defend Taiwan from a military attack from the PRC become law?" — [Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com/question_clusters/)
- The method was "developed… in a research study sponsored and validated by the US Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity from 2011-2015" (paraphrase of search snippet). — [Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com/question_clusters/)
- Tetlock, conversation with Open Philanthropy, 2016 (older; paraphrase with verbatim fragments): "Bayesian question clusters" means that "Open Philanthropy could make specific predictions about what it would expect to observe in the relative near-term, both within grantee programs and in relevant external circumstances and developments, if these were on the longer-term trajectory that Open Philanthropy anticipates." Example: "come up with a set of indicators that one would expect to observe if artificial intelligence (AI) were advancing quickly enough to significantly disrupt labor markets", such as "new AI feats (such as the recent AlphaGo victory, or the existence of driverless Ubers by the end of 2017)"; recommendation to create "a substantial set of questions that will resolve within the next six months", some within one month, for faster feedback. — [Open Philanthropy, Tetlock 2016-03-08](https://www.openphilanthropy.org/wp-content/uploads/Philip_Tetlock_03-08-16_public.pdf)
- Tetlock, Karvetski, Satopää, Chen (Futures & Foresight Science, 2024), abstract (verbatim): "The current study gives geopolitical forecasters a sporting chance by focusing on slow-motion variables with low base rates of change… we propose adversarial collaborations in which clashing schools of thought negotiate Bayesian reputational bets on divisive issues and use Lakatosian scorecards to incentivize the honoring of bets." (This is the cluster idea applied to long-range disputes: each side names near-term observables that would move its view.) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/fufsci/v6y2024i1ne157.html)
- ForecastBench's "combination questions" are the benchmark's own decomposition device: "The best forecast of whether the S&P500 will reach an all-time high and whether the U.S. will enter an economic recession must account for the likely strong correlation between these events." LLMs "perform poorly on these combination questions" (superforecasters 0.076 vs GPT-4o 0.130 Brier). — [ForecastBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19839)
- FutureSearch's anti-triviality devices (paraphrase): a forecast verifier produces a probability to identify trivial questions; the desiderata include "non-extreme base rates", "difficult" (performance should improve with intelligence and effort) and "independent outcomes". — [arXiv 2601.22444](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22444)
- Failure modes catalogue for AI forecasting questions (LessWrong "How to write good AI forecasting questions", older; paraphrase with examples): *Ambiguity* (terms like "AGI"); *Underspecification*; *Spurious resolution* (a battleship accidentally hitting a fishing boat resolving an "incidents" question); *Trivial pathways* ("a superhuman Angry Birds agent question that hinges on whether anyone decides to work on it, not actual progress"); *Failed parameter tuning* (asking about 100% of jobs automatable "creates boring edge cases, whereas 99.999% would be more interesting"); *Non-incentive-compatible* questions. — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yy3FCmdAbgSLePD7H)
- Manifold's one-line coarse-vs-specific example (verbatim): "'Will an AI model score over 90% on benchmark X by date Y?' beats 'Will AI get smarter this year?'" — [Manifold](https://manifold.markets/community-guidelines/creator-guide)
- FinanceHarness (arXiv 2607.27853, July 2026) is the one benchmark found that generates the *analyst-level* question rather than a settlement contract: "Given a (situation, cutoff-date) pair, an LLM generates the benchmark record without category priming: the prompt does not provide a topic, sector, or reasoning taxonomy." The record is "an analyst-style question, a reference investment thesis, and a two-tier rubric", where "Pre-cutoff criteria test facts findable before the cutoff date; post-cutoff criteria test outcomes verifiable only after the cutoff date… it separates evidence retrieval from forward-looking synthesis while keeping both sides auditable." (verbatim) — [FinanceHarness](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27853)

### Inferences
- The accepted granularity rule across sources: the *unit that gets a probability and a resolution date* is narrow (one metric, one period, one source); the *unit that carries relevance* is the cluster, and the cluster is explicitly multi-angle (Good Judgment lists five angles). For a thesis-extraction gold, this suggests two levels of gold: the thesis-level question (graded for relevance, not resolvability) and 3–6 dated sub-questions (graded for resolvability), with an explicit link from each sub-question to the thesis it would move.
- A one-line-item question is not wrong by these standards; it is incomplete. Good Judgment's tariff-rate question is "rigorous" and would be rejected only as a *standalone* answer to "what will market conditions be". So the gold should penalize a lone line item only when the cluster it belongs to is missing, not when it appears as a member.
- FinanceHarness's pre-/post-cutoff split is the closest published analogue to "do not conflate plan with realised": the same question gets criteria for what was knowable at the document date and criteria for what later resolved it.

### Gaps
- No source gives a numeric rule for cluster size or for how resolved sub-questions mathematically update the parent question (Tetlock's 2016 conversation does not; the 2024 paper's abstract does not). Good Judgment's public page shows one six-question cluster only.
- Tetlock & Karvetski's full 2024 text was not read (paywalled); only the abstract.

## Key question 3. Which rubric dimensions recur when humans grade an analyst's thesis (materiality, falsifiability, variant vs consensus, evidence attribution, correct period, not conflating plan with realised)? Catalogue of 2024–2026 benchmarks.

### Takeaway
Across eleven 2024–2026 benchmarks the recurring dimensions are: comprehensiveness/coverage of the material drivers (often with sector-KPI hard caps), insight/analytical depth, assumption quality and justification, evidence traceability/claim verifiability (with hallucination rate measured), numerical accuracy vs a reference or a professional range, recommendation/direction accuracy, and readability/instruction following. "Variant vs consensus" and "period discipline" appear only indirectly (Finance Agent Benchmark's "Beat or Miss" category; FinanceHarness's pre-/post-cutoff split; Polymarket's "as reported… revisions not considered"). Nearly every benchmark now uses an LLM judge against an expert-written or expert-reviewed rubric, with human experts validating a sample; reported judge–human agreement ranges from Spearman 0.54 to κ≈0.81.

### Cited Findings (one bullet block per benchmark)

**ForecastBench — Karger et al., FRI, arXiv 2409.19839 (Sept 2024; ICLR 2025)**
- Unit: a probability on a binary question; metric Brier "(f−o)²", "a score of 0.25 is associated with the uninformed forecast of 0.5". Labels come from reality (resolution), not from graders. Human subset of 200 questions: superforecasters 0.096, public 0.121, Claude-3.5-Sonnet 0.122; "Expert forecasters outperform the top-performing LLM (p-value <0.001)." Humans excluded from combination questions "due to cost considerations". — [ForecastBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19839); [ICLR page](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/ea74e45a229dac70b5b63b28d8934db6-Abstract-Conference.html)

**Prophet Arena — arXiv 2510.17638 (Oct 2025; ICLR 2026)**
- Unit: probability per Kalshi market. Metrics: Brier, Expected Calibration Error, and "Market Return" from a utility-maximizing bet against market prices. Failure modes (verbatim fragments): "Event recall varies by topics and models" — for weather and politics "almost all of their recalled events were false" for some models; "Sources drive LLMs' forecasts to be more conservative than markets"; markets "quickly surpass LLMs in short-term accuracy" near resolution ("slower information aggregation compared to markets"). — [Prophet Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17638v2)

**Finance Agent Benchmark — Vals AI, arXiv 2508.00828 (Aug 2025)**
- Unit: an answer to one of "537 expert-authored questions"; "a taxonomy of nine financial task categories, developed in consultation with experts from banks, hedge funds, and private equity firms." Categories with counts: Quantitative Retrieval 102 (19%), Qualitative Retrieval 97 (18%), Numerical Reasoning 83 (15%), Complex Retrieval 29 (6%), Adjustments (GAAP vs non-GAAP) 43 (8%), **Beat or Miss (guidance vs actual) 69 (13%)**, Trends 33 (6%), Financial Modeling 47 (9%), Market Analysis 34 (6%). — [Finance Agent Benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.00828)
- Labeling: experts drafted questions; "Each question underwent peer review by a different expert"; authors did a final standardization review. Rubrics: "GPT-4o" extracted initial rubrics, then "manually reviewed by the authors"; a "contradiction rubric" checks "whether any part of the generated answer conflicts with the expert's answer"; conjunction rules combine criteria into a correct/incorrect verdict. "No inter-rater agreement metrics or judge error rates are reported." Best model: "even the best-performing model (OpenAI o3) achieved only 46.8% accuracy at an average cost of $3.79 per query." Human experts 16.8 min/task vs o3 3.1 min. Model failure modes: too little exploration, ineffective tool use, strategy inflexibility, hallucinated documents. — [Finance Agent Benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.00828); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00828)

**IPO Finance Agent — arXiv 2606.23032 (June 2026)**
- Unit: answers to 70 due-diligence questions on the SpaceX S-1. Rubrics auto-generated: "Candidate facts are extracted from model answers, consolidated into draft criteria, then automatically audited for omissions, hallucinations, mistiered items, and redundancy, with LLM feedback driving iterative repair"; "Human experts only review final rubrics before deployment." No agreement numbers in the abstract. Failure: the prior harness "basically failed to deliver any output related to the SpaceX S-1 filing, due to document length." — [arXiv 2606.23032](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23032)

**FinRpt — arXiv 2511.07322 (Nov 2025)**
- Unit: a full equity research report (Chinese–English; 6,825 reports on CSI800 stocks, Sept 3–Nov 5 2024; test 652). 11 metrics: 5 basic (CompletionRate, Accuracy of buy/sell rating, BERTScore, ROUGE-L, NumberRate) and 6 LLM-judged: Financial Numeric ("precision of the data presented and the depth of the financial analysis"), News, CMI (management/trajectory/market insight), Invest ("whether the investment recommendations are grounded in thorough, logical, and well-reasoned analysis"), Risk, Writing. Judge GPT-4o in pairwise comparison with "order swapped" to "eliminate position bias". Human check: 50 pairs, judge agreed with the consensus of three senior analysts on 45/50 = 90%. Dataset-quality check: 30 generated + 30 expert reports rated by three senior analysts, Fleiss' κ = 0.86. — [FinRpt](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07322v1)

**Deep FinResearch Bench — arXiv 2604.21006 (Apr 2026)**
- Unit: an AI-generated equity research report. Three dimensions: Qualitative Rigor (comprehensiveness, coherence, assumption quality, analytical depth); Quantitative Accuracy (forecast SMAPE; valuation hit rate/MAE/bias; recommendation directional accuracy); Claim Verifiability (factuality rate, hallucination rate, non-verification rate). Scale: four points (Poor/Fair/Good/Excellent) with hard caps — "missing sector-specific KPIs or unlinked valuation drivers automatically cap comprehensiveness at 'Fair'". Labeling: eight domain experts annotated a small validation set, ≥2 annotators per report; judges GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4; GPT-5 "aligned closely with human experts" at 75–85% pairwise agreement. Failure modes: weak sector-KPI coverage, weak assumption justification, little scenario/sensitivity analysis, poor revenue/EPS forecasting; hallucination rates 11–34% across systems. — [Deep FinResearch Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21006v1)

**FinResearchBench II — arXiv 2607.12252 (July 2026)**
- Unit: a financial deep-research report answering one of 104 real user queries. Rubric dimensions "comprehensiveness, insight, instruction following, and readability". Gold rubrics are *consensus-derived*: 14,450 candidate rubrics generated from the multi-product report set; "A rubric passes the consistency filter only if all three judges agree on every one of the 10 reports"; "A rubric is considered distinguishable if at least one report receives a majority-yes label and at least one report receives a majority-no label"; "3,687 rubrics pass the strict consistency filter and 2,600 remain distinguishable." Of candidates, "1,056 are always-yes and only 31 are always-no" (so distinguishability filtering removes always-pass rubrics). Scoring is binary per rubric; product score = pass rate over the gold set. — [FinResearchBench II](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12252v2)
- Labeling: validation by three human financial analysts (~10 years' experience) vs a three-LLM panel (GPT-5.4, Claude-Opus-4-7, Gemini-3-Pro-Preview); full evaluation by the LLM panel. Agreement: on jointly unanimous items "98.67% (2,829/2,867; Cohen's κ=0.9733)"; across all validation items majority-label agreement "90.42% (κ=0.8076)"; "LLM–human agreement (mean agreement 0.848, mean κ=0.694) exceeds human–human agreement (mean agreement 0.811, κ=0.569–0.678, mean κ=0.622)"; within-model stability: "90.99% of rubric evaluations are fully consistent across all three runs." Self-preference check by leave-one-judge-out: "removing the Gemini judge leaves the Gemini product unchanged at rank 3." — [FinResearchBench II](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12252v2)

**FinanceHarness / FinanceGym — arXiv 2607.27853 (July 2026)**
- Unit: an analyst-style question + reference investment thesis + two-tier rubric (pre-cutoff facts vs post-cutoff outcomes). Each criterion scored 0–4: "0 (not addressed), 1 (mentioned), 2 (partial), 3 (substantive), and 4 (fully grounded)." Expert annotators "score question feasibility and clarity and mark each rubric item as feasible or infeasible with a short justification"; "82% professional-annotation pass rate". — [FinanceHarness](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27853)

**GAUGE — "Grading Agent-Built Financial Models Without a Golden Answer", arXiv 2607.24889 (July 2026)**
- Unit: an agent-built financial model workbook + memo + assumptions file. 56 facets across five pillars (input comprehension, model construction, forecast reasoning, valuation, communication & auditability): 29 deterministic code checks (54% of outcomes; balance-sheet identity, cash-flow tie-outs, formulas not hardcodes), 4 rule-based range facets, 23 rubric facets judged by "A frozen LLM judge (Claude Opus 4.8)" with k=5 votes, majority rule, ties to the lower score; rubric scores 0/1/2. — [GAUGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24889)
- Acceptable-range construction ("envelopes"): strict band from the reference workbook's own multi-method valuations widened by observed cross-analyst dispersion (e.g. "147 bp" median WACC difference), 10th–90th percentile of the same GICS industry group, and 65 held-out multi-covered companies; "A near band widens the strict band by half of its own width on each side," scoring 2 (excellent) or 1 (pass). — [GAUGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24889)
- Judge validation: "Two independent re-judgings of the same cells agree at Kendall τ=0.944 with a 2.2% facet flip rate." "The judge matches the expert consensus on 86.7% of cases (weighted κ=0.81), while the experts agree with each other on 89.4% (κ=0.85)." Agreement falls by facet type: "mechanical-adjacent facets (91.2%) to assumptions (85.0%) to valuation (82.9%)." A rival judge (GPT-5.6) gave "73.5% exact agreement", "quadratic-weighted κ=0.675", but "agent ordering is preserved (Kendall τ=0.857)." Human baseline: 55 participants (12 senior, 18 junior analysts, 25 students). — [GAUGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24889)

**CM-LRS — "Capital Markets LLM Reliability Score", arXiv 2607.21340 (July 2026)**
- Seven 0–5 dimensions: "factual accuracy, evidence traceability, numerical consistency, workflow completeness, source discipline, decision usefulness, and reviewability/auditability", anchored to "signals that regulated reviewers employ". Judged by "four independent LLM judges spanning three model families". "Decision Usefulness shows the widest cross-model dispersion of any dimension (4.0 points on issuer profiling) and top-tier inter-judge agreement (mean r = 0.52)." Gap vs frontier "concentrates on retrieval (2.23) and synthesis (2.15), not extraction (0.84)." — [CM-LRS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21340)

**BigFinanceBench — Rogo, arXiv 2606.03829 / announced 2026-05-27**
- Unit: an open-ended financial-research answer (928 questions: capital structure, M&A, valuation, private capital, metrics, earnings analysis, scenario forecasting) graded on the full derivation. "The 52 practitioners who wrote the questions also wrote the rubrics, and a panel of 12 senior reviewers stress-tested every one." Judges: Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.7, scores averaged across judge families to limit self-preference (paraphrase of aiwiki summary). "Across all evaluated models, rubric scores exceed final-answer accuracy by roughly 16 percentage points." Top three "separated by less than 0.3 percentage points overall"; best agent 58.8% (third-party summary). No human–judge agreement reported. — [Rogo announcement](https://rogo.com/news/introducing-the-big-finance-benchmark); [aiwiki summary](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/bigfinancebench); [agentmarketcap summary](https://agentmarketcap.ai/blog/2026/07/22/bigfinancebench-financial-research-agent-benchmark)

**Business-disciplines benchmark — arXiv 2607.16057 (July 2026)**
- Unit: an answer to a business-school case question; rubric is an "equally-weighted checklist" of binary criteria derived from the instructor solution; Standard score = share of criteria met; "Complete Answer" requires all. Top model 88.4% Standard but 49.6% Complete — "high partial credit frequently coexists with unmet completeness requirements." Human graders: three annotators with business-school grading experience, four-stage "staged unblinding" (usability check → blinded rubric authoring and grading → view automated rubric and LLM score → compare). On ten triple-graded items: human–LLM Spearman ρ = 0.54; 100% rated automated rubrics acceptable/mainly acceptable; 96% rated LLM scores acceptable; 74% preferred automated scores or judged them equivalent. Failure mode: "incomplete satisfaction of multi-part rubrics", "analytically strong drafts" missing required elements. — [arXiv 2607.16057](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16057v5)

**Bridgewater AIA Labs — "Learning to replicate expert judgment in financial tasks" (undated blog, ~2025)**
- Unit: a document on "Six information filtering tasks drawn from investors' daily workflows" (classification/relevance). Metric: "Accuracy—the percentage of documents that were correctly labeled according to our investors. For classification tasks, we also calculated the F1 score." Frontier models started at "a mere ~50% accuracy", prompting reached "mid-70s", trained model 84.7% vs frontier 78.2%. **ResearchRubrics — arXiv 2511.07685 (Nov 2025; general deep research, not finance-only)**
- "2,500+ expert-written, fine-grained rubrics to assess factual grounding, reasoning soundness, and clarity", "over 2,800+ hours of human labor"; leading agents "achieve under 68% average compliance with our rubrics, primarily due to missed implicit context and inadequate reasoning about retrieved information." — [ResearchRubrics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07685)

### Inferences
- Dimension frequency across the eleven finance-facing sets: coverage/comprehensiveness (7), evidence traceability/verifiability or hallucination rate (6), numerical accuracy (6), insight/analytical depth (5), assumption quality (3), recommendation direction (3), readability/instruction following (5), auditability (2). The dimensions the task asked about but no benchmark names as a scored dimension: *falsifiability of the thesis*, *variant vs consensus*, and *plan vs realised*. The nearest proxies are Finance Agent Benchmark's "Beat or Miss" category (guidance vs actual) and FinanceHarness's pre-/post-cutoff rubric tiers.
- Two designs avoid a single gold answer and are reusable for "right questions at the right granularity": GAUGE's envelope bands (an answer passes if it falls inside a strict band derived from cross-analyst dispersion, near-band for partial credit), and FinResearchBench II's consensus-derived rubric set (a criterion is gold only if judges agree on it everywhere and it separates at least one good from one bad report). The latter directly operationalizes "gold is a set of acceptable answers".
- Rubric vs final-answer scoring diverge by ~16 points (Rogo) and 88% vs 50% (business cases): partial-credit rubrics reward "analytically strong drafts" that miss a required element. A gold for question extraction should therefore have must-have items (the thesis-moving questions) scored as a conjunction and nice-to-have items scored additively.

### Gaps
- No benchmark found grades *question extraction* itself (did the system ask the right questions of a document); all grade answers, reports or models. FinanceHarness is the closest (it generates the analyst question and thesis but then grades agents' answers).
- Finance Agent Benchmark, IPO Finance Agent and BigFinanceBench report no judge–human agreement.
- Vals AI's own site pages and Expected Parrot's finance material were not found by search; Hebbia and Brightwave publish no evaluation methodology found here (only a marketing comparison page).

## Key question 4. What inter-annotator agreement do these tasks achieve, and how do they handle "acceptable alternatives"?

### Takeaway
Reported human–human agreement on analyst-style grading is moderate-to-substantial: κ 0.57–0.68 on rubric labels (FinResearchBench II), κ 0.85 / 89.4% on 0/1/2 facets (GAUGE), Fleiss κ 0.86 on report quality (FinRpt), with agreement degrading from mechanical to judgement facets (91.2% → 82.9% in GAUGE). The NLP meta-literature shows the field's norm is three annotators with majority vote and that only 46% of papers even report agreement. "Acceptable alternatives" are handled by ranges/envelopes, by rubric *sets* instead of one answer, by soft labels, and by expert adjudication of disagreements.

### Cited Findings
- "Illusions of the Gold Standard" (arXiv 2606.07936, June 2026; survey of *CL 2023–2025 papers, 284 manually annotated of 1,891 eligible; paraphrase with numbers): 77% of papers report the number of annotators; median 3 annotators (32% use exactly 3, 20% use 2); only 46% report inter-annotator agreement (51% among multi-annotator papers); median sample for computing agreement 190 items; among papers reporting agreement roughly 35% reach moderate or better and ~55% do not report strength; only 30% say how disagreements were resolved — majority vote 38%, averaging 24%, consensus 17%. Recommendations: report guidelines, annotator count, sample size, agreement; justify dimension choice; raise rigor because human protocols now serve as gold for meta-evaluating LLM judges. — [arXiv 2606.07936](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07936)
- Uma, Fornaciari, Hovy, Paun, Plank, Poesio, "Learning from Disagreement: A Survey", JAIR 2021 (older; paraphrase of search snippet): humans disagree on both objective tasks (POS tagging) and subjective ones (NLI, image classification); with many high-quality judgments per item, "training directly with soft labels achieved better results than training from aggregated or even gold labels"; even without a gold standard, "it is still essential to reach consensus on how to evaluate models". — [JAIR](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/12752)
- GAUGE: experts agree with each other "on 89.4% (κ=0.85)"; judge vs expert consensus 86.7% (weighted κ 0.81); agreement by facet type 91.2% mechanical → 85.0% assumptions → 82.9% valuation. Acceptable alternatives: envelope bands from cross-analyst dispersion and industry 10th–90th percentiles; near band = strict band widened by half its width each side; k=5 judge votes, ties resolved downward. — [GAUGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24889)
- FinResearchBench II: human–human mean κ 0.622 (range 0.569–0.678), LLM–human mean κ 0.694; a rubric is gold only if "all three judges agree on every one of the 10 reports" and it is "distinguishable". Authors' caution (verbatim): "LLM unanimity is not merely a reproducibility indicator; in this validation subset, it is also a strong proxy for label-level agreement with expert judgments." — [FinResearchBench II](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12252v2)
- FinRpt: three senior analysts, Fleiss κ 0.86 on 60 reports; judge–consensus agreement 45/50. — [FinRpt](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07322v1)
- Deep FinResearch Bench: eight experts, ≥2 per report, judge–human agreement 75–85% on a four-point scale with hard caps. — [Deep FinResearch Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21006v1)
- Business-disciplines benchmark: three graders, ten triple-graded items, human–LLM Spearman 0.54; staged unblinding so human rubrics are written before seeing the automated rubric. — [arXiv 2607.16057](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16057v5)
- FutureSearch: human accept/soft-reject/hard-reject on 149 questions = 75.2/16.8/8.1%; one expert forecaster as resolution ground truth on 100 questions. — [arXiv 2601.22444](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22444)
- FinanceHarness: experts mark each rubric item "feasible or infeasible with a short justification"; 82% pass. — [FinanceHarness](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27853)
- Finance Agent Benchmark: author → peer expert → paper-author review, three stages, no agreement number. — [Finance Agent Benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.00828)
- Bridgewater: vendor non-expert labels were "often wrong"; disagreements between model and labeler routed to in-house experts. — [Bridgewater](https://www.bridgewater.com/aia-labs/learning-to-replicate-expert-judgment-in-financial-tasks)
- Rogo: 52 practitioner authors, 12 senior reviewers stress-tested every rubric; criteria weighted 1–10, ~17 per question. — [Rogo](https://rogo.com/news/introducing-the-big-finance-benchmark)
- Generic gold-corpus practice (paraphrase of search snippets): two annotators plus a third adjudicator; iterative guideline refinement on a double-annotated sample until agreement stabilizes; "disagreeing annotations could both be seen as correct given the inherent subjectivity… new rules were created to arrive at one consistent label for edge cases." — [HackerNoon gold-standard write-up](https://hackernoon.com/this-is-how-we-created-gold-standard-data-for-developing-the-nlp-pipelines)

### Inferences
- A realistic target for an analyst-thesis gold is κ≈0.6–0.7 between two experienced analysts on rubric-item labels, higher (κ≈0.85) only when items are near-mechanical; judgement items (valuation, assumptions) will sit at the low end. Designs that reported higher human agreement did so by making items binary and concrete.
- The three working patterns for "acceptable alternatives" are: (1) a *band*, not a point (GAUGE); (2) a *set of criteria any good answer must satisfy*, keeping only criteria that experts agree on and that discriminate (FinResearchBench II); (3) *soft labels* / disagreement kept as signal (Uma et al.). For question extraction, (2) maps to "gold = the set of thesis-moving questions a document supports, each with the sub-questions that would resolve it"; a system output passes an item if it names a question that resolves the same uncertainty, not the same words.
- Protocol pieces that recur: ≥2 independent annotators per item, a third adjudicator, the rubric authored by the same experts who wrote the question (Rogo), stress-test by a senior panel, and blinding of human graders to model output until they have committed their own rubric (staged unblinding).

### Gaps
- No finance paper reports agreement specifically on *which questions matter* (materiality judgement); all numbers are on report/answer grading.
- Specific partial-credit schemes for forecasting-question gold (e.g. half credit for right metric wrong period) were not found in any source.

## Key question 5. What are documented pitfalls of using an LLM as the judge of thesis quality (self-preference, verbosity bias, agreement with the generator)?

### Takeaway
The general LLM-judge literature (2024–2025) documents twelve bias families, with position and verbosity the most damaging and self-enhancement traced to perplexity (judges prefer text that looks like their own). The finance benchmarks add domain-specific pitfalls: judge accuracy degrades as facets move from mechanical to judgement (GAUGE), cross-provider judges agree only ~73% exactly, rubric generators over-produce always-pass criteria (FinResearchBench II: 1,056 always-yes vs 31 always-no), rubric partial credit inflates scores ~16 points over final-answer accuracy (Rogo), and judge agreement is lowest exactly on "decision usefulness" (CM-LRS). Mitigations in use: order swapping, multi-family judge panels with leave-one-out checks, frozen judge versions with repeated votes, binary criteria, and distinguishability filtering.

### Cited Findings
- CALM framework, "Justice or Prejudice? Quantifying Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge" (arXiv 2410.02736, Oct 2024): 12 biases (verbatim definitions): Position ("LLM judges exhibit a propensity to favor one answer at certain position over others"); Verbosity ("LLM judges favor longer responses, even if they are not as clear, high-quality"); Compassion-Fade; Bandwagon ("stronger preference to the majority's beliefs"); Distraction ("more attention to irrelevant or unimportant details"); Fallacy-Oversight ("may ignore logical errors in reasoning steps"); Authority ("more credibility to statements made by authority figures"); Sentiment; Diversity; Chain-of-Thought; Self-Enhancement ("LLM judges may favor the answers generated by themselves"); Refinement-Aware ("Telling the model that this is a refined result will lead to different evaluations"). Position bias is the most severe: robustness rates drop below 0.5 with three or four answer options; alignment-style datasets are more vulnerable than fact datasets, i.e. judges are least robust when quality differences are small (paraphrase). — [arXiv 2410.02736](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736)
- "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge" (arXiv 2410.21819, Oct 2024; paraphrase of snippet): LLMs assign higher evaluations to outputs with lower perplexity than humans do; "the self-preference bias exists because LLMs prefer texts more familiar to them." — [arXiv 2410.21819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819)
- Surveys (paraphrase): "A Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge" (arXiv 2411.15594) and "LLMs-as-Judges" (arXiv 2412.05579) catalogue position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias; a 2024 study across six judges and 22 tasks found position bias "is most pronounced when the quality gap between candidates is small"; order rearrangement is the standard mitigation. — [arXiv 2411.15594](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15594); [arXiv 2412.05579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05579)
- "Self- and Other-Labels Induce Bidirectional Bias in LLM Judges" (arXiv 2608.18091, Aug 2026): telling a judge which output is its own (or another model's) shifts judgements in both directions (title-level claim; full text not read). — [arXiv 2608.18091](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18091)
- GAUGE (finance): judge–expert agreement falls from 91.2% on mechanical-adjacent facets to 82.9% on valuation; a rival-provider judge reaches only "73.5% exact agreement" (κ 0.675) though rankings hold (τ 0.857); repeated judging of the same cells flips 2.2% of facets; mitigation is a frozen judge version, k=5 votes, majority with ties to the lower score. — [GAUGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24889)
- FinResearchBench II: generated rubrics skew toward always-pass ("1,056 are always-yes and only 31 are always-no"), so unfiltered rubric sets reward every report; mitigation is the distinguishability filter; self-preference tested by leave-one-judge-out ("removing the Gemini judge leaves the Gemini product unchanged at rank 3"); single-judge run-to-run stability 90.99%. — [FinResearchBench II](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12252v2)
- FinRpt: pairwise judging with "order swapped" to "eliminate position bias". — [FinRpt](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07322v1)
- CM-LRS: "Decision Usefulness" has the widest cross-model dispersion and inter-judge agreement of only mean r = 0.52; four judges from three families used to average out family bias. — [CM-LRS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21340)
- Rogo: two judges from different families averaged "to limit self-preference" (paraphrase); "rubric scores exceed final-answer accuracy by roughly 16 percentage points." — [aiwiki](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/bigfinancebench); [Rogo](https://rogo.com/news/introducing-the-big-finance-benchmark)
- Business-disciplines benchmark: human graders write their rubric *before* seeing the automated one (staged unblinding) specifically so human gold is not anchored on model output; human–LLM ρ only 0.54 even though 96% of LLM scores were rated acceptable. — [arXiv 2607.16057](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16057v5)
- Deep FinResearch Bench: judge choice matters — only GPT-5 "aligned closely with human experts" (75–85%); hallucination rates of 11–34% in generated reports mean a judge without source access cannot verify claims. — [Deep FinResearch Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21006v1)
- Bridgewater: the inverse pitfall — cheap human labels were "often wrong" and model disagreement was used to find them. — [Bridgewater](https://www.bridgewater.com/aia-labs/learning-to-replicate-expert-judgment-in-financial-tasks)
- Prophet Arena (judging forecasts, not text): models recall false events in some domains ("almost all of their recalled events were false"), so a model used to *resolve* or *judge* questions can hallucinate the ground truth. — [Prophet Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17638v2)

### Inferences
- For a thesis-granularity gold, the biases most likely to bite are: verbosity (a system listing more questions looks more "comprehensive"); always-pass rubric items (criteria every output trivially satisfies); and judgement-facet drift (the judge is least reliable exactly on "is this the question that moves the stock"). The documented counters are: binary criteria that discriminate on real outputs, a cap on the number of questions credited, judges from two families with leave-one-out, order swap for any pairwise step, and a frozen judge version with repeated votes.
- Several teams now report LLM–human κ above human–human κ (FinResearchBench II 0.694 vs 0.622). This is not evidence the judge is better; it reflects conservative filtering to items judges agree on, as the authors themselves say. A gold built that way measures only the easy part of the task.

### Gaps
- No paper quantifies "agreement with the generator" specifically for *investment theses* (i.e. whether a judge rewards theses that match the consensus it would itself write). Self-enhancement is measured only on generic QA/alignment sets and via rank-stability checks in finance.
- The full texts of 2410.21819, 2411.15594, 2412.05579 and 2608.18091 were not read; only abstracts/snippets.
