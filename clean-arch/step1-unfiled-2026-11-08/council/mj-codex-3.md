**I agree with the direction, but the rule needs two changes before I can call it defensible:** establish that the control actually exercises the memory question, and specify and check the interval calculation at sparse counts.

For example, if neither version opens a memory-price question in any fresh answer, a zero-variance calculation could produce a difference of zero and allow the candidate through. That would tell us neither version exercised the behavior we wanted to protect.

The exact changes are:

1. **Add an exercised-control rule:** at least one usable c8 answer among the 54 fresh sends must open a memory-price question. Otherwise the question comparison is **inconclusive**. Apply the same principle to retention: if the required statements cannot be judged in the usable c8 answers, retention is inconclusive. Continue counting unusable answers conservatively for the decision, but report their actual number separately; do not describe an unusable c8 answer as having really opened or retained anything.

2. **Name the exact interval implementation and validate it before sending.** “Mantel–Haenszel risk difference with a 90% interval” does not by itself identify how the interval handles zero or near-zero counts. Freeze the variance and interval method, then check simulated coverage and the chance of an erroneous proceed **at the −22.2-point boundary**, including sparse and unequal rates across the three stored states. If the method cannot produce a valid interval for the observed counts, the result is inconclusive.

The reported simulation supports the proposed sample size under its stated equal-rate, independent-send assumptions. I have not independently reproduced it, and it does not establish performance at the decision boundary or across differing states.
